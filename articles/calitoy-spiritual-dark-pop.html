The Sacred and the Dark: Calitoy's Spiritual Undercurrent

Risen Today · August 31, 2026 · Spiritual Art

There is a tradition in spiritual writing that goes back to the desert fathers and mothers of the early centuries. They did not find God in the light. They found God in the wilderness, in the dry silence, in the places where comfort could not survive. They understood that the sacred and the dark are not opposites. They are collaborators. And Calitoy, a dark pop artist working in San Diego, is carrying that understanding into sound.

Eighty-six tracks on SoundCloud are the evidence. But the spiritual current running through them is not in the production alone or the melody alone. It is in the way these songs approach the human condition. They do not preach. They do not resolve. They sit with the difficulty and let the sitting itself become a form of prayer.

Faith as a Room You Enter

Consider the track titles from the upcoming Kurced debut album, arriving September 2026. "Locked Out" is the spiritual experience of separation, the feeling of being outside the door that others walk through. "Self" is the contemplative turn inward, the examination that every wisdom tradition demands. "Be Alone" is the monastic cell, the deliberate solitude that clears the noise and makes room for the voice that speaks in silence.

These are not religious songs in the conventional sense. They are spiritual in the deeper sense, the sense that the mystics meant when they wrote about the dark night of the soul. The dark night is not punishment. It is purification. It is the process by which everything that is not essential burns away, and what remains is what was always true.

"Lantern in the Fog" is not a song about finding your way. It is a song about the light that stays when you cannot see where you are going.

Redemption Without Easy Answers

"Lioness Lost Hard Tides" and "Lioness Lost Unfinished Tides" are two versions of the same spiritual ordeal. One is the completed storm. The other is the storm that has not yet passed. Together they say something that most spiritual music is afraid to say: that the redemption is real and the struggle is also real, and the two do not cancel each other out. You can be saved and still be in the water. You can be held and still be lost.

This is the kind of theological honesty that most contemporary spiritual art avoids. We want the breakthrough without the breakdown. We want the resurrection without the tomb. Calitoy gives us both, in the same breath, in the same tracklist, without pretending that the darkness is over when it is not.

Read the list as a spiritual journey and it maps to the contemplative path with surprising precision. "Locked Out" is alienation. "Todos Americanos" is the shared condition. "Lioness Lost Hard Tides" is the first crisis. "Lioness Lost Unfinished Tides" is the crisis that keeps going. "Self" is the turning inward. "Be Alone" is the practice of solitude. "Lantern in the Fog" is the first sign of grace. "For" is the dedication of the self to something larger. "Always There" is the recognition of the divine presence. "Not You" is the shedding of what is not yours to carry.

Survival as Sacred Text

"Always There" and "Not You" sit at the end of the album like two pillars. One says what stays. The other says what leaves. The spiritual life is built on exactly this distinction. Faith is not the absence of doubt. It is the presence of something that stays when doubt has taken everything else. And "Not You" is the boundary that makes the staying possible. You cannot hold what is sacred if you are still carrying what is not.

The XCalitoy vinyl pressing is, in this context, an act of permanence. It is the decision to make these spiritual explorations physical, to press them into a medium that endures. There is something liturgical about vinyl. You place the needle. You commit to a side. You listen in order. It is a ritual, and the music it carries is ritual music for people who do not call it that.

The Darkness That Illuminates

Calitoy's dark pop is not nihilism. It is the tradition of the mystics who went into the dark to find what the light could not show them. St. John of the Cross wrote an entire book about the dark night. The Sufis spoke of the moth that flies into the flame. The Buddha sat in the shadow of a tree. The pattern is ancient and it is consistent: the sacred is not afraid of the dark. It lives there.

What Calitoy has built across eighty-six SoundCloud tracks and ten debut album tracks is a spiritual practice rendered in sound. It does not require belief in any specific tradition. It requires only the willingness to sit in the dark long enough to notice that the dark is not empty. It is full. And what it is full of is what every contemplative tradition has always pointed toward: the presence that stays when everything else has passed.
